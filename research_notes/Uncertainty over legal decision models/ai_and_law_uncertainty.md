## Q1. Which works put probabilities on legal rules, interpretations or argument acceptance, and where do the probabilities come from (elicited, learned, assumed)?

### Takeaway
Most probabilistic work in AI & Law is about **facts and evidence** (category a: Bayesian networks with expert-elicited, openly subjective numbers). A small line, mainly Riveret, Rotolo, Sartor and Prakken, plus the general probabilistic-argumentation literature (Hunter, Thimm, Li/Oren/Norman, Dung and Thang), puts probabilities on **whether an adjudicator will accept a rule or premise, or whether an argument is acceptable** (category b). In that line the probabilities are almost always **assumed or illustrative**. The founding paper names "where do the numbers come from?" as an open problem and suggests court statistics as the answer. Learned probabilities appear only for simulated learning agents (reinforcement learning, Boltzmann machines), not for real legal corpora.

**(b) Uncertainty about which rule or interpretation applies, or whether an argument is accepted**
- Riveret, Rotolo, Sartor, Prakken & Roth, "Success chances in argument games: a probabilistic approach to legal disputes", JURIX 2007, pp. 99-108. The probability is placed on **acceptance by the adjudicator**, Pr(accepted(q)), not on the truth of a state of affairs, and the authors argue that "on our interpretation… it makes sense to assign probabilities to normative statements or rules." In the worked example, the rule that only exogenous events count as force majeure is "not so sure that the judge will accept it, since it is a debated one". Probabilities of premise acceptance propagate through a Prakken-Sartor dialogue tree to a probability of success (0.4941 in the example) and then to an expected utility of suing. How the numbers are obtained: "A probability distribution is assumed." The conclusion asks "where do the numbers for the probabilities come from? Statistics could help by providing the number of same-level courts that accepted a specific rule, or by showing how a specific judge decided in the past", and suggests sensitivity analysis when a number is not verifiable. [V-abs, full text] — [PDF (Prakken's page)](https://webspace.science.uu.nl/~prakk101/pubs/jurix07.pdf); [SSRN DOI](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.1100672)

### Inferences
- In category (b) the probabilities are almost always placeholders: assumed (Riveret et al. 2007), illustrative (Riveret et al. 2020), or learned by simulated agents (2012, 2015, 2019). I found no AI & Law paper that estimates Pr(adjudicator accepts rule r) from real decisions, even though Riveret et al. 2007 proposed exactly that ("number of same-level courts that accepted a specific rule"). That proposed estimator is, in effect, a jurisdiction-level acceptance rate, close to a random-effect "jurisdiction implements interpretation k" parameter in a hierarchical model.
- The **acceptance-by-adjudicator semantics** (Riveret et al. 2007) is the conceptual move most useful for latent decision models. It sidesteps the question of whether a legal rule can be "true" by putting probability on an observable institutional act (acceptance or implementation). That matches "how jurisdictions implement it" (the process model) better than "what the statute requires".
- Prakken (2018) shows that arguments carry hidden independence assumptions that can conflict across arguments. A posterior over decision models built from several LLM formalizations would face the same issue if each formalization were treated as an independent argument.
- The (a)/(b) boundary is explicit in Dung & Thang (2010): law is crisp, facts are probabilistic. The project's target, probability over the law itself, sits on the less developed side of that line.

### Gaps
- Abstracts were not available for Riveret et al. 2012 (AI & Law 20) or Riveret et al. 2019 (JAAMAS), so exactly what the agents learn, and from which reward signal, is not verified here.
- I found no AI & Law paper that elicits from experts the probabilities of acceptance for rules or interpretations, which would be the rule-level analogue of BN elicitation (Renooij-style elicitation is cited only for evidence BNs in Vlek et al. 2014).
- The ICAIL 2007 bibliographic details for Roth et al. could not be confirmed.

## Q2. Is there work on multiple competing formalizations of the same statute, and on how to weigh them?

### Takeaway
Yes, but it is fragmentary. (i) Allen & Saxon showed early on that legal rules have multiple *structural* interpretations and built an interpretation-assistance tool (MULTINT). (ii) Rules-as-Code experiments (Witt, Huggins, Governatori & Buckley) measure how independent legally trained coders diverge when encoding the same Act. (iii) Catala-related work detects ambiguities statically. (iv) LLM-to-logic pipelines compare formalizations against expert gold encodings. Only the evidence literature (Neil et al. 2019) actually **weights** competing models, through Bayesian model averaging. Nothing I found weights competing *statutory* formalizations probabilistically.

### Inferences
- The Witt et al. experiments are the nearest empirical analogue to "several LLM formalizations of one statute". The divergence sources they name (interpretive complexity, granularity, language expressiveness) suggest that a posterior over latent decision models needs **formalizer-specific effects**: a granularity or representation-choice effect separate from the substantive interpretive choice. A Dawid-Skene or IRT-style rater model, or latent-space position plus rater bias in the spirit of Hoff, Raftery and Handcock, fits that structure better than majority voting.
- Allen & Saxon's "multiple structural interpretations" suggests a discrete hypothesis space (alternative parse and scope structures of a rule) that could be enumerated before inference, as a mixture over structural readings, while open-textured predicates would be continuous or latent.
- AI & Law treats competing interpretations argumentatively: canons attack and support, and preferences resolve conflicts. None of the interpretation-argumentation papers verified here attaches probabilities. Riveret et al. (2018, 2020) supply the machinery that could do so.

### Gaps
- I found no paper that collects *multiple LLM* formalizations of the same provision and weights or aggregates them probabilistically. Horner et al. (2025) compare LLM configurations against an expert gold standard rather than modelling disagreement as uncertainty. This should be checked again with a full JURIX 2024-2025 and ICAIL 2025 table-of-contents scan, which I could not do.
- How AGATHA and Bench-Capon & Sartor (2003) score competing theories (explanatory power, simplicity, and so on) is not verified here because the abstracts were inaccessible.

## Q3. Has anyone validated these probabilities (calibration against outcomes or experts)?

### Takeaway
There is essentially **no calibration of rule-level or argument-acceptance probabilities against real outcomes** in AI & Law. What exists: (i) validation of *structure* against human intuitions (Lagnado et al.; Polberg & Hunter); (ii) model weighting by fit to observed trial facts (Neil et al. 2019); (iii) accuracy-only validation of learned outcome predictors (IBP, VJAP, ECtHR prediction), often weakened by temporal leakage; (iv) recent LLM-interpretation studies benchmarking output *distributions* against human surveys (Engel & McAdams) or showing instability (Blair-Stanek & Van Durme; Purushothama et al.); (v) one 2026 preprint that reports ECE for legal outcome prediction.

### Inferences
- The project's planned calibration (posterior intervals checked against administrative records or held-out jurisdictions) would be new relative to AI & Law. The closest precedent for checking an LLM-derived distribution over meanings against an external human reference is Engel & McAdams (LLM sample distribution vs. survey).
- The instability findings (Blair-Stanek & Van Durme; Purushothama et al.) mean that repeated LLM samples are **not** exchangeable draws from interpretive uncertainty. Prompt format is a nuisance factor that should be modelled explicitly, for example as a formalization-protocol random effect, or the posterior will mix prompt variance with legal ambiguity.
- Medvedeva et al.'s temporal-split result is a warning for any decision-model posterior validated on outcomes: held-out evaluation should be forward in time or by jurisdiction.
- Saka (2026), if it holds up, shows that naively multiplying LLM-derived likelihoods miscalibrates. That supports hierarchical pooling over naive Bayes-style fusion of multiple LLM formalizations.

- I found no study calibrating probabilistic-argumentation acceptance probabilities against actual court outcomes or expert panels.
- VJAP's calibration, as opposed to accuracy, was not verifiable from the abstract.
- Engel & McAdams' quantitative agreement with the Tobia survey is not in the abstract excerpt I retrieved.

## Q4. How does AI & Law treat open texture, vagueness and ambiguity, and what does that imply for representing uncertainty about legal concepts?

### Takeaway
The community's main tradition represents open texture through **cases and factors** (HYPO, CATO, IBP, CABARET, GREBE) and, more recently, through **formal models of precedential constraint** (Horty's reason and result models and their hierarchical, dimensional and inconsistent-case-base extensions). These give *constraint sets* (which outcomes are forced or permitted) rather than probabilities. The newer work handles uncertainty in two ways: set-valued uncertainty about facts with "stability" and "relevance" (Odekerken, Bex & Prakken), and tolerance of inconsistent precedent (Canavotto; Di Florio, Dong & Rotolo; Fungwacharakorn et al.). Both can be read as non-probabilistic supports onto which a posterior could be placed.

### Inferences
- The precedential-constraint models define, for each new case, the set of outcomes consistent with precedent. A posterior over latent decision models can use this as a hard or soft support constraint: zero or low prior mass on decision models that violate binding precedent. The inconsistent-case-base generalisations (Canavotto 2022; Di Florio et al. 2024) are what make this robust to real, noisy decision records.
- Odekerken et al.'s "stability/relevance" is the set-valued counterpart of posterior predictive certainty and value of information. Placing a distribution over the possible values turns "stability" into Pr(outcome unchanged) and "relevance" into expected information gain. This is a natural bridge from the AI & Law formalism to a Bayesian process model.
- Canavotto & Horty's hierarchy result (flattening loses or creates constraint) is a warning against collapsing intermediate predicates (for example, "habitable", "substandard") into one latent variable. The latent decision model should keep the intermediate concept nodes that statutes and opinions actually use.
- "Ambiguity collapse" (Gur-Arieh et al.) names the failure mode of taking one LLM formalization as the statute's meaning. Several formalizations plus explicit posterior spread is the remedy.

## Q5. Has anyone inferred the rule or its interpretation from data with quantified uncertainty?

### Takeaway
There are induction methods (defeasible-logic theory induction, association-rule mining of the ratio decidendi, argument-based ML, LLM rule induction), but they output **point theories** with accuracy scores, not posteriors. The clearest *Bayesian* rule-inference work is in the adjacent normative multi-agent systems community (Cranefield et al. 2016, Bayesian norm identification). Work using known ground truth (Bench-Capon 1993; Steging et al. 2021) shows high-accuracy learners often learn the **wrong rule**, which bears directly on identifiability. Hoff-like latent-variable models of courts exist in political science (ideal points), not in AI & Law.

## Q6. What recent (2022-2026) neurosymbolic or LLM-plus-logic work in ICAIL, JURIX or AI & Law addresses uncertainty?

### Takeaway
Recent LLM-plus-logic work concentrates on (i) **translating statutes into executable logic** (Prolog, Defeasible Deontic Logic, Catala) and measuring accuracy against gold encodings, and (ii) **documenting LLM instability and non-robustness** on legal interpretation.
