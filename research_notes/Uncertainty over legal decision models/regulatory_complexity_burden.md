## 1. Quantitative legal-complexity metrics (text, structure, network, entropy, logical/decision-tree form)

### Takeaway
Most quantitative legal-complexity work measures size, hierarchy, cross-reference networks and linguistic entropy. Bourcier & Mazzega (2007) split measures into structure-based and content-based; Katz & Bommarito (2014) refined this into structure, language and interdependence. Measures based on decision logic (default logic, Catala, Halstead/McCabe-style program metrics) exist but are rarely tested against real-world outcomes. The main exception is Colliard & Georg (2025), who tested computer-science complexity measures of financial rules against experiments and a compliance-cost survey. I found no study that computes decision-tree depth or entropy for a statute and relates it to processing times.

### Inferences
- Measures fall into three families. (i) Size and text: tokens, pages, entropy, compression. (ii) Structure and network: hierarchy depth, cross-reference graphs, clustering. (iii) Logic and program: default-logic formalization, cyclomatic or Halstead-type operator/operand counts, decision-tree depth and leaves. The project's decision-tree measures belong to family (iii), which has the least evidence against outcomes.
- Colliard & Georg (2025) is the closest methodological precedent for "do CS complexity measures correlate with outcomes." Their outcomes are experimental task performance and survey-reported compliance cost, not administrative timing data. A California permitting-time test would extend their approach to real processing records.
- Bayesian CART (posterior over trees) and Rashomon-set work turn "one LLM tree" into a distribution over trees. Complexity would then be reported as a posterior (for example, the distribution of depth, leaf count or path entropy), which speaks directly to Dan's reliability concern.

## 2. Administrative-burden measurement frameworks (Herd & Moynihan; PRA burden hours; administrative-data designs)

### Takeaway
The standard framework is Moynihan, Herd & Harvey's (2015) learning, psychological and compliance costs, developed in Herd & Moynihan (2018). It treats burden as a deliberate policy choice. Empirical burden work mostly uses quasi-experiments on administrative data: office closures, interview timing, rule reductions, notice simplification. Federal Paperwork Reduction Act (PRA) burden-hour estimates are a separate, agency-reported measure that finance economists have recently used as a regressor.

### Cited Findings
- Moynihan, Herd & Harvey, "Administrative Burden: Learning, Psychological, and Compliance Costs in Citizen-State Interactions," JPART 25(1):43–69 (2015; online 2014). Burden is "a function of learning, psychological, and compliance costs," and its level and distribution between state and individual are often "a function of deliberate political choice" — [DOI 10.1093/jopart/muu009](https://doi.org/10.1093/jopart/muu009)
- Herd & Moynihan, *Administrative Burden: Policymaking by Other Means* (Russell Sage Foundation, Dec. 2018; ISBN 978-0-87154-444-5; 360 pp.). Argues that burdens are deliberate policy choices that fall hardest on the disadvantaged — [Russell Sage Foundation](https://www.russellsage.org/publications/administrative-burden)
- Halling & Bækgaard, "Administrative Burden in Citizen–State Interactions: A Systematic Literature Review," JPART 34(2):180–195 (2024; online 2023). Reviews 119 articles and working papers since 2012. The evidence supports the claims that burdens are consequential, distributive and constructed, and it identifies where causal evidence is thin — [DOI 10.1093/jopart/muad023](https://doi.org/10.1093/jopart/muad023)
- Herd, DeLeire, Harvey & Moynihan, "Shifting Administrative Burden to the State: The Case of Medicaid Take-Up," PAR 73(s1) (2013). Mixed methods. Wisconsin process changes reduced individual burden and increased Medicaid take-up — [DOI 10.1111/puar.12114](https://doi.org/10.1111/puar.12114)
- Fox, Stazyk & Feng, "Administrative Easing: Rule Reduction and Medicaid Enrollment," PAR 80(1):104–117 (2020; online 2019). State-level panel of simplified enrollment and renewal procedures, 2008–2017. Rule reduction conditions changes in Medicaid enrollment. This is a direct "number of rules → take-up" design — [DOI 10.1111/puar.13131](https://doi.org/10.1111/puar.13131)
- Homonoff & Somerville, "Program Recertification Costs: Evidence from SNAP," AEJ: Policy 13(4):271–298 (2021). Later recertification interview slots reduce recertification success by 22%. A quarter of those who lose benefits stay off SNAP for more than a year. The marginal disenrolled case is as needy as the average participant — [DOI 10.1257/pol.20190272](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20190272)
- Deshpande & Li, "Who Is Screened Out? Application Costs and the Targeting of Disability Programs," AEJ: Policy 11(4):213–248 (2019). SSA field-office closures cause a persistent 16% fall in disability recipients but only a 10% fall in applications. Congestion matters more than travel cost — [DOI 10.1257/pol.20180076](https://doi.org/10.1257/pol.20180076)
- Bhargava & Manoli, "Psychological Frictions and the Incomplete Take-Up of Social Benefits," AER 105(11):3489–3529 (2015). IRS field experiment with 35,050 filers. - Finkelstein & Notowidigdo, "Take-Up and Targeting: Experimental Evidence from SNAP," QJE 134(3):1505–1556 (2019). Randomized information and assistance for 30,000 elderly likely-eligible non-enrollees. Only 6% of the control group enrolled — [DOI 10.1093/qje/qjz013](https://doi.org/10.1093/qje/qjz013)
- Heinrich, "The Bite of Administrative Burden: A Theoretical and Empirical Investigation," JPART 26(3):403–420 (2016; online 2015). The citation is confirmed; I did not retrieve the abstract — [DOI 10.1093/jopart/muv034](https://doi.org/10.1093/jopart/muv034)
- Benzarti, "How Taxing Is Tax Filing? Using Revealed Preferences to Estimate Compliance Costs," AEJ: Policy 12(4):38–57 (2020). - Kalmenovitz, "Regulatory Intensity and Firm-Specific Exposure," RFS 36(8):3311–3347 (2023). Builds a firm-level measure of the "cost of compliance with all federal paperwork regulations" from administrative (PRA) data and machine learning. ### Inferences
- Burden studies identify effects from changes in *process* (office closures, interview slot timing, rule reductions, simpler notices), not from a measured complexity score of the rule text. Rule complexity is still not quantified as an exposure in this literature, which is the gap a formalized decision-tree measure could fill.
- The learning/psychological/compliance split lines up with the project's habit of keeping elapsed waiting, service time and payer hours separate. Permitting timing records mostly show elapsed agency time, not applicant compliance hours.
- PRA hours are agency estimates, not measured outcomes. Kalmenovitz shows they can work as an exposure variable, but they share the measurement-error problems of any self-reported burden figure.

### Gaps
- No verified source on the accuracy of PRA burden estimates (GAO critiques were not retrieved).
- No verified validated survey instrument for administrative burden. Psychometric measures exist in the literature reviewed by Halling & Bækgaard, but I did not open specific instruments.
- Christensen et al. (2020, PAR) and Bækgaard & Tankink (2022) were not verified in this pass.

## 3. Empirical links: local permitting process and rooftop-solar timing and cost (California emphasis)

### Takeaway
There is solid evidence, much of it from California, that local permitting process quality affects residential PV prices and development times. The benchmark is Dong & Wiser (2013): 44 California cities, about 24 days (25% of median) shorter development times and $0.27–0.77/W lower prices in the most streamlined cities. Automated permitting (SolarAPP+) cuts permit review to the same day and the permit-to-inspection time by about 12–14.5 business days. California SB 379 now requires automated permitting in most jurisdictions. All of these studies measure process with scores or tool adoption, not with the formal complexity of the governing code.

### Cited Findings
- Dong & Wiser, "The impact of city-level permitting processes on residential photovoltaic installation prices and development times: An empirical analysis of solar systems in California cities," Energy Policy 63:531–542 (2013). Uses more than 3,000 PV installations in **44 California cities in 2011**, city permitting "scores" from DOE's Rooftop Solar Challenge, CSI data and the Census. The most favorable permitting cuts prices by **$0.27–$0.77/W (4–12% of median CA prices)**. The most streamlined practices "may shorten development times by around **24 days** on average (**25% of the median development time**)." The development-time models are "less robust" — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/enepol/v63y2013icp531-542.html); [DOI 10.1016/j.enpol.2013.08.054](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2013.08.054)
- Burkhardt, Wiser, Darghouth, Dong & Huneycutt, "Exploring the impact of permitting and local regulatory processes on residential solar prices in the United States," Energy Policy 78:102–112 (2015). Combines two independent local-process scoring systems with the largest U.S. installed-price dataset. Permitting procedures alone account for about **$0.18/W** between least and most favorable jurisdictions ($0.14/W across the middle 90%, about $700 or 2.2% on a 5-kW system). Including other local regulatory processes, the gap grows to **$0.64–$0.93/W** (at least $2,500, or 8%) — [LBNL publication page](https://energyanalysis.lbl.gov/publications/exploring-impact-permitting-and-local); [DOI 10.1016/j.enpol.2014.12.020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2014.12.020)
- Seel, Barbose & Wiser, "An analysis of residential PV system price differences between the United States and Germany," Energy Policy 69:216–226 (2014). In 2012 U.S. residential PV cost a median $5.29/W against $2.59/W in Germany, mostly because of soft costs. These include permitting, interconnection and inspection, and the U.S. also has "longer project-development times" — [IDEAS/RePEc abstract](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/enepol/v69y2014icp216-226.html); [DOI 10.1016/j.enpol.2014.02.022](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2014.02.022)
- O'Shaughnessy, Nemet, Pless & Margolis, "Addressing the soft cost challenge in U.S. small-scale solar PV system pricing," Energy Policy 134:110956 (2019). A literature synthesis. Soft costs are lower for larger systems, for experienced installers, in more competitive markets, and "in markets with less onerous permitting requirements" — [DOI 10.1016/j.enpol.2019.110956](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.enpol.2019.110956)
- Nemet, O'Shaughnessy, Wiser, Darghouth, Barbose, Gillingham & Rai, "Characteristics of low-priced solar PV systems in the U.S.," Applied Energy 187:501–513 (2017). The citation is confirmed; I did not retrieve the abstract — [DOI 10.1016/j.apenergy.2016.11.056](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.apenergy.2016.11.056)
- Gillingham et al., *Deconstructing Solar Photovoltaic Pricing: The Role of Market Structure, Technology, and Policy* (LBNL/OSTI report, 2014) — [DOI 10.2172/1166986](https://doi.org/10.2172/1166986)
- NREL SolarTRACE: Cruce, Lara, Frey, Dalecki & Cook, *Residential and Small Commercial Solar PV and Storage Permitting, Inspection, and Interconnection Timelines: A Retrospective Review (2017–2023)*, NREL/TP-6A20-92539 (Nov. 2025). Uses time-stamped installer data covering **22% of U.S. residential solar installs since 2017** and tracks permitting changes at about 4,000 AHJs. A typical solar permit takes **1–2 weeks** of AHJ review, ranging from same-day to **6+ weeks**. Contract-to-PTO times averaged **4.5–6 months in 2023**, a month longer than in 2019, and the recent increases are mostly in the design and installation phases. PV-plus-storage averaged **8–9 months** — [OSTI 3011864](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/3011864); [DOI 10.2172/3011864](https://doi.org/10.2172/3011864)
- NREL, *SolarAPP+ Performance Review (2023 Data)*, NREL/TP-6A20-89618 (June 2024). "A typical SolarAPP+ project is permitted and inspected **14.5 business days sooner** than traditional projects (based on median timelines)." It saved about 15,400 hours of AHJ staff time and reduced installation costs by about 2–13% — [OSTI 2373099](https://www.osti.gov/biblio/2373099); [DOI 10.2172/2373099](https://doi.org/10.2172/2373099)
- *SolarAPP+ Performance Review (2024 Data)*, NLR/TP-6A20-95050 (Jan. 2026; NREL now publishes as the National Laboratory of the Rockies). Deadlines are 9/30/2023 for jurisdictions over 50,000 people and 9/30/2024 for smaller ones. Cities under 5,000 and counties under 150,000 (with their cities) are exempt. Jurisdictions must report permitting metrics annually to the CEC. These details come from search snippets of the CEC page and Assembly committee analysis; I did not fetch the full text — [CEC SB 379 reporting page](https://www.energy.ca.gov/publications/2022/residential-solar-permit-reporting-senate-bill-379-sb-379-wiener-chapter-356); [Assembly analysis PDF](https://alcl.assembly.ca.gov/sites/alcl.assembly.ca.gov/files/SB%20379%20%28Wiener%29.pdf)

### Inferences
- The solar literature sets plausible effect sizes. In California cities, moving from the most onerous to the most streamlined permitting is worth weeks (about 24 days, 25% of median), not months. Automation compresses AHJ review from about a week to zero. A complexity measure that predicts differences of this order across about 500 jurisdictions would be consistent with prior evidence.
- Every solar study measures process through *scores* (Rooftop Solar Challenge, SolarABCs-type) or *tool adoption*, not through the structure of the governing rule text. Under §65850.5 and SB 379 the state rule is largely uniform, so variation across solar jurisdictions comes mainly from local implementation (checklists, inspection practice, local amendments), not from statutory logic. That matters for identification: the statute's decision-tree complexity barely varies across California solar AHJs unless local ordinances are formalized too.
- SolarAPP+ comparisons are SolarAPP+ versus traditional permits *within adopting AHJs*. Installers and projects self-select into the pathway, so the 12–14.5-day gaps are descriptive contrasts, not randomized effects. (Inference from the report design; the reports' own caveats were not extracted in detail.)
- SB 379's annual CEC reporting is a possible statewide, jurisdiction-level source of solar permitting metrics alongside SolarTRACE.

### Gaps
- I did not retrieve the abstract of the Gillingham et al. Energy Journal (2016) version or the full text of Nemet et al. (2017).
- No verified study links SB 379 compliance status to timing outcomes. The law is recent, and nothing turned up in this pass.
- I did not verify an "O'Shaughnessy et al." paper that is specifically about local regulation and solar prices, beyond the 2019 soft-cost review and the 2017 Nemet et al. paper.

## 4. Empirical links: housing and ADU permitting, regulatory-stringency indices, and delay (California emphasis)

### Takeaway
Housing research finds that discretionary review, more approval bodies and higher regulatory-index scores are associated with longer approvals, less permitting and higher land prices. Examples: Los Angeles by-right projects are permitted 28% faster; WRLURI's most-regulated quartile reports 8.4-month reviews against 3.7 months in the least regulated. But survey-based "process" measures are unreliable and endogenous. Delay is itself a component of the WRLURI, so "the index predicts delay" is partly built in. For ADUs, California APR records show long application-to-permit times despite a 60-day statutory clock, and the data have large gaps in dates.

### Cited Findings
**Indices and how they relate to delay**
- Gyourko, Saiz & Summers, "A New Measure of the Local Regulatory Environment for Housing Markets: The Wharton Residential Land Use Regulatory Index," Urban Studies 45(3):693–729 (2008). Survey of more than 2,000 jurisdictions. The West, and California especially, ranks among the most regulated — [DOI 10.1177/0042098007087341](https://doi.org/10.1177/0042098007087341)
- Gyourko, Hartley & Krimmel, "The local residential land use regulatory environment across U.S. housing markets: Evidence from a new Wharton index," JUE 124:103337 (2021). Survey of about 2,472 communities, with more than 800 matched to the 2006 survey. The **Approval Delay Index (ADI)** is one of the subindices of WRLURI2018. It averages survey-reported review times in months across by-right and non-by-right single- and multifamily projects, building permits, and subdivisions. Bottom-quartile (least regulated) communities average **3.7 months (111 days)**, the interquartile group **5 months**, and the top quartile **8.4 months (252 days)**, with a 6.9-month SD and an upper tail of **18–24 months**. Top-quartile places need about 3.22 approving entities without a zoning change and 3.69 with a variance. San Francisco and New York metros are the most regulated — [Wharton-hosted JUE PDF](https://realestate.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/04/w835.pdf); [IDEAS](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/juecon/v124y2021ics009411902100019x.html); [NBER w26573](https://www.nber.org/papers/w26573)
- Lewis & Marantz, "What Planners Know: Using Surveys About Local Land Use Regulation to Understand Housing Development," JAPA 85(4):445–462 (2019). Nine California planner surveys, 1988–2018. Two surveys of the same municipalities months apart gave "strikingly different responses to similar inventory questions," which casts doubt on reliability. *Subjective* measures predict later housing production, "unlike counts of purportedly objective measures." The authors call for state collection of harmonized zoning data — [DOI 10.1080/01944363.2019.1643253](https://doi.org/10.1080/01944363.2019.1643253)
- Monkkonen, Lens & Manville, "Built-Out Cities? How California Cities Restrict Housing Production Through Prohibition and Process," Terner Center Land Use Working Paper (Feb. 2020). Uses the Terner California Residential Land Use Survey and Housing Element zoned-capacity data on permitting from 2013 to 2017. A *prohibition* index is strongly associated with less permitting, especially multifamily. A *process* index (parking, hearings, fees and similar) is **uncorrelated with permitting**, though its base data are incomplete. "Places with more development are more likely to report delays and other process complexities," which makes process measures endogenous — [Terner Center PDF](https://ternercenter.berkeley.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/MLM-Built-Out-Cities-2020.pdf)

**Process, discretion and time to permit**
- Manville, Monkkonen, Gray & Phillips, "Does Discretion Delay Development? The Impact of Approval Pathways on Multifamily Housing's Time to Permit," JAPA 89(3):336–347 (2023; online 2022). About 350 Los Angeles multifamily projects permitted from January 2018 to March 2020. They exploit the Transit-Oriented Communities by-right provision. **By-right projects were permitted 28% faster** than discretionary ones, with controls, and had **lower variance** in approval time — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/taf/rjpaxx/v89y2023i3p336-347.html); [DOI 10.1080/01944363.2022.2106291](https://doi.org/10.1080/01944363.2022.2106291)
- Kok, Monkkonen & Quigley, "Land use regulations and the value of land and housing: An intra-metropolitan analysis," JUE 81:136–148 (2014). San Francisco Bay Area. "Cities that require a greater number of independent reviews to obtain a building permit or a zoning change have higher land prices, ceteris paribus" — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/juecon/v81y2014icp136-148.html); [DOI 10.1016/j.jue.2014.03.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jue.2014.03.004)
- Jackson, "Do land use regulations stifle residential development? Evidence from California cities," JUE 91:45–56 (2016). California city panel, 1970–1995. With two-way fixed effects, each additional regulation cuts residential permits by about **4%**, and fixed-effects estimates are 50–75% smaller than pooled ones. Some individual regulations have large *positive* effects — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/juecon/v91y2016icp45-56.html); [DOI 10.1016/j.jue.2015.11.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jue.2015.11.004)
- Mayer & Somerville, "Land use regulation and new construction," Regional Science and Urban Economics 30(6):639–662 (2000). The citation is confirmed. IDEAS shows "No abstract," so the commonly cited finding that delay-type regulation affects construction more than fees is **[UNVERIFIED]** here — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/regeco/v30y2000i6p639-662.html); [DOI 10.1016/s0166-0462(00)00055-7](https://doi.org/10.1016/s0166-0462(00)00055-7)
- Quigley & Raphael, "Regulation and the High Cost of Housing in California," AER 95(2):323–328 (2005). Citation confirmed — [DOI 10.1257/000282805774670293](https://doi.org/10.1257/000282805774670293)
- Kahn, Vaughn & Zasloff, "The housing market effects of discrete land use regulations: Evidence from the California coastal boundary zone," J. Housing Econ. 19(4):269–279 (2010). Citation confirmed — [DOI 10.1016/j.jhe.2010.09.001](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jhe.2010.09.001)

**ADU-specific (California)**
- California's ADU timing rule is now **Gov. Code §66317** (ADU provisions were moved out of §65852.2 into §66310 et seq.; last amended by Stats. 2025, ch. 520, SB 543). Applications are approved ministerially without discretionary review or a hearing. The agency must decide completeness within **15 business days** and approve or deny within **60 days** of a complete application when a dwelling already exists on the lot — [leginfo §66317](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?sectionNum=66317&lawCode=GOV)
- Underriner, "Comparing ADU Permitting Time Inside and Outside the Coastal Zone," Terner Center blog (Mar. 21, 2024). Uses HCD **Annual Progress Report** data from 2018 to 2022, linking Table A (applications) to Table A2 (permits) by APN, to measure mean days from planning application to building permit. **Los Angeles County: 260 days inside the Coastal Zone vs. 147 outside. Orange County: 233 vs. 101** (both p<0.01). San Diego shows no significant difference. On data quality: the APR has about **60,000 ADU projects with valid permit dates, but only about 29,000 have both application and permit dates**, so usable records may be biased. A robustness check with HCD-cleaned data showed the same pattern with moderately lower magnitudes — [Terner Center](https://ternercenter.berkeley.edu/blog/adus-coastal-zone/)
- HCD's *Housing Element Annual Progress Report (APR) Data by Jurisdiction and Year* is on the state open-data portal. APRs are required by Gov. Code §65400 for every city and county — [data.ca.gov](https://data.ca.gov/dataset/housing-element-annual-progress-report-apr-data-by-jurisdiction-and-year) (metadata confirmed through the CKAN API)
- Terner Center/CCI, "Reaching California's ADU Potential" (Aug. 28, 2020). ADU permits rose from 5,911 in 2018 to 15,571 in 2019, and completions from 1,984 to 6,668. The main barrier identified is financing — [Terner Center](https://ternercenter.berkeley.edu/blog/reaching-californias-adu-potential-progress-to-date-and-the-need-for-adu-finance/)
- Chapple et al., first statewide ADU owner survey (Terner/CCI, Apr. 22, 2021). Median ADU construction cost is $150,000 ($250/sf), and "difficulty obtaining permits" is a significant barrier — [Terner Center](https://ternercenter.berkeley.edu/blog/cci-adu-survey/)
- Greenberg et al., "ADUs for All" (Terner/CCI, Aug. 2022). Homeowner focus groups describe "complicated contracting and permitting processes" as barriers and recommend streamlining permits and cutting fees — [Terner Center](https://ternercenter.berkeley.edu/blog/adu-equity-barriers/)
- Marantz, Tanrisever & Kim, "Where Do Accessory Dwelling Units Expand Housing Supply? Evidence from California" (SSRN, 2026). Uses parcel- and individual-level administrative data to test whether permitted ADUs are actually lived in — [DOI 10.2139/ssrn.6926256](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.6926256)

### Inferences
- The WRLURI "approval delay" component is a survey-reported review time, so any correlation between WRLURI and delay is partly mechanical. Monkkonen et al. and Lewis & Marantz show that survey process measures are unreliable and reverse-caused: high-development places report more delay. An exposure computed from the statute or ordinance, as in this project, is not affected by that reporting endogeneity. That is a concrete argument for formal rule-derived complexity.
- Manville et al.'s 28% by-right effect, with lower variance, is the cleanest California estimate of a structural process difference (ministerial vs. discretionary branch) on time to permit. In decision-tree terms it is one binary split at the root, and it suggests branch type may matter as much as depth.
- For ADUs, state law imposes a uniform ministerial pathway with 15-business-day and 60-day clocks. Observed application-to-permit means of about 100–260 days in Southern California therefore far exceed the statutory clock (the clock starts at a *complete* application, so the two are not strictly comparable). Cross-jurisdiction variation probably comes from local ordinance layers, overlays such as the Coastal Zone, and capacity, not from the state statute's logic alone.
- APR date missingness (about half of ADU records lack paired dates) is a selection problem any timing model must handle. Records with dates may differ systematically from those without.

### Gaps
- Not verified: Mangin (exclusionary zoning), O'Neill/Gualco-Nelson/Biber entitlement-timeline studies (Berkeley/UCLA, 2018–2019), and Mawhorter & Reid (2018) Terner survey documentation. Crossref did not return them, and the search budget was used up.
- I found no verified peer-reviewed paper by Manville or Monkkonen specifically on ADU permitting timelines.
- I found no verified study relating the *text or logic complexity* of local ADU ordinances to ADU permitting time across California jurisdictions.

## 5. Formal or machine-readable representations of regulation related to outcomes (RegData, PRA text, procedure counts)

### Takeaway
Counting machine-readable "restrictions" (RegData), pages (Mulligan & Shleifer), procedures (Djankov et al.) or Federal Register fragmentation (Kalmenovitz et al.) and relating the counts to outcomes is an established approach. Results are mixed. RegData does not explain falling business dynamism (Goldschlag & Tabarrok) but is linked to slower growth in a structural model (Coffey et al.). Procedure counts track time and cost of entry across countries. These are volume and count measures, not decision-logic measures.

### Cited Findings
- Al-Ubaydli & McLaughlin, "RegData: A numerical database on industry-specific regulations for all United States industries and federal regulations, 1997–2012," Regulation & Governance (2017). Text analysis counts binding constraints ("restrictions") in the CFR by industry (NAICS 2–4 digit) and agency — [DOI 10.1111/rego.12107](https://doi.org/10.1111/rego.12107); [SSRN WP DOI 10.2139/ssrn.2099814](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.2099814)
- McLaughlin, Sherouse & Potts, "RegData: Australia" (2019). Applies the RegData method to Australian legislation and regulation — [DOI 10.2139/ssrn.3420352](https://doi.org/10.2139/ssrn.3420352)
- Goldschlag & Tabarrok, "Is regulation to blame for the decline in American entrepreneurship?," Economic Policy 33(93):5–44 (2018). "Rising federal regulation cannot explain secular trends in economic dynamism" (RegData plus measures of dynamism) — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/ecpoli/v33y2018i93p5-44..html); [DOI 10.1093/epolic/eix019](https://doi.org/10.1093/epolic/eix019)
- Coffey, McLaughlin & Peretto, "The cumulative cost of regulations," Review of Economic Dynamics 38:1–21 (2020). A Schumpeterian growth model fitted with RegData 2.2 for 22 industries, 1977–2012. Regulation reduced growth by about 0.8% per year since 1980 — [DOI 10.1016/j.red.2020.03.004](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.red.2020.03.004)
- Mulligan & Shleifer, "The Extent of the Market and the Supply of Regulation," QJE 120(4):1445–1473 (2005). States with larger populations have more pages of legislation and adopt particular laws earlier — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v120y2005i4p1445-1473..html); [DOI 10.1162/003355305775097579](https://doi.org/10.1162/003355305775097579)
- Djankov, La Porta, Lopez-de-Silanes & Shleifer, "The Regulation of Entry," QJE 117(1):1–37 (2002). For 85 countries, records the **number of procedures, official time and official cost** to start a firm. Heavier entry regulation goes with more corruption and larger unofficial economies but not better public or private goods — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/oup/qjecon/v117y2002i1p1-37..html); [DOI 10.1162/003355302753399436](https://doi.org/10.1162/003355302753399436)
- Kalmenovitz, Lowry & Volkova, "Regulatory Fragmentation," Journal of Finance 80(2):1081–1126 (2025). Full-text Federal Register measure of how many agencies oversee one issue. Fragmentation raises costs, lowers productivity, profitability and growth, deters entry, and increases small-firm exit, mainly through inconsistencies between agencies — [DOI 10.1111/jofi.13423](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.13423)
- Kalmenovitz (2023): PRA-filing-based regulatory intensity predicts firm costs, investment and hiring (see Section 2) — [DOI 10.1093/rfs/hhad001](https://doi.org/10.1093/rfs/hhad001)
- Colliard & Georg (2025): computer-science complexity measures of financial regulation, validated against experiments and a compliance-cost survey (see Section 1) — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/jfinec/v174y2025ics0304405x25001941.html)

### Inferences
- The count-based tradition (restrictions, pages, procedures) has the outcome-linkage designs this project needs: panel fixed effects, structural growth models, cross-jurisdiction regressions. But its exposures are volume measures. Goldschlag & Tabarrok's null result shows that volume does not automatically predict outcomes, which supports measuring *logical* structure (branching, exceptions, path length) rather than size.
- Djankov et al.'s "number of procedures" is the nearest cross-national analogue to a step count or path length in a process decision tree. It was designed together with time and cost measures, which matches the project's split between steps, money and time.
- The formal-representation literature (RegData, Catala, Lawsky) and the timing literature (permitting studies) have not been joined. I found no published work that formalizes permit rules (for ADU or solar) and relates their logical complexity to processing times.

### Gaps
- I did not verify State RegData (Mercatus state-level datasets) and could not find a study that uses state or local RegData to predict permitting times.
- I did not verify the World Bank Doing Business "Dealing with Construction Permits" indicator (procedures, time, cost) or its 2021 discontinuation; flag it if the report cites it.
- I found no verified study that uses codified local permit rules (for example, machine-readable municipal code) as an exposure for permitting time.
