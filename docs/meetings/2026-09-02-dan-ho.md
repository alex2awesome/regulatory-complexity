## His critiques (design-changing)

1. **Lurking discretion inside objective conditions**: "age can be defined 5 different ways" (ReadyReturn); "was the unit previously permitted" was a clerk's call. Discretion lives in *inputs/extractors*, not only predicates → add an input-level looseness pass. Related: Braithwaite rules-vs-standards counterexample — standards sometimes outperform rules; squeezing out discretion isn't automatically good (current "abundance" moment = legal system writing ever more rules).
2. **Redundancy is not waste**: conditions that add no information given the others may exist for salience — something concrete for applicants and "a defense to the examiner." Information-theoretic redundancy ≠ welfare redundancy. Constraint for `simplify.py`. (The dispute-rate correlation test was ALEX's idea in the meeting, not Dan's — attribute accordingly.)

3. **Burden is under-defined** (Dan): is burden compiling the paperwork, or what happens when paperwork is missed — rework rounds and project delay? Delay costs are real burden and hard to know ex ante for one project; the answer is expectations from administrative data (rounds-of-review distributions, permit timestamps), not per-project prediction.

4. **"Condition" is under-defined** (Dan, per Alex's read): multi-part decisions can sit inside one condition objective; a single condition can imply multiple steps. → split the representation into DECISIONS (choice points with an owner and outcome effect) composed of CONDITION atoms (span-pinned testable propositions) with explicit aggregation logic — including point/score systems (NY/SD sheets are weighted sums with grade thresholds, which a binary tree can't represent).

## Alex's abstraction question (answered in session)

Law has abstraction (cross-references = function calls) but lacks what makes CS abstraction work: (1) a single reliable implementation — legal "libraries" are implemented by humans at call time, one implementation per official, so the call hides *variance* (= our looseness band), and dependencies are unpinned ("latest edition of the UBC"); (2) information hiding from the right party — legal abstraction hides internals from the *drafter* while the regulated person still pays for them. Slogan: abstraction without reliable executors is delegation, and delegation is discretion. Dan's redundancy point is the flip: concrete spelled-out conditions are law refusing abstraction for the reader's sake.
