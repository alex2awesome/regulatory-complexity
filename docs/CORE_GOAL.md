# Core goal

Living document. Stated by Alex, 2026-09-26. Every workstream (Solar, CalFresh,
CCW, synthetic worlds, the toy statute) should be able to say which part of
this goal it serves. If it cannot, it is probably a digression.

## The lever

**Our end goal is better-written statutes.** The lever we have for change is
the drafting of statutes (and the rules that implement them). Everything we
measure should ultimately help a drafter answer: *if I write this statute
differently, how does the burden on the public change?*

Measuring agencies, forums or permits for their own sake is not the goal. The
measurements matter because they connect statutory text to real-world burden.

## The conceptual frame

```
statute text
  -> decision model      (what the law requires to be decided; read from the text)
  -> process models      (how each jurisdiction actually implements each decision step)
  -> observations        (admin records, applicant reports, agency web pages;
                          partial, noisy, censored, selectively reported)
```

- **Decision model.** Derived by reading the statute. Its decision steps
  (d-steps) are fixed and known with high confidence. Each is tagged
  **concrete** (the text says what happens) or **vague** (the text sets a
  standard, e.g. "determine it will not create a nuisance", and leaves the
  procedure open). Deadlines, conditions and branches in the text are part of
  the decision model.
- **Process models.** Each jurisdiction refines each d-step into process steps
  (p-steps): who does what, in what order, with what waiting time, effort and
  fees. Concrete d-steps admit few refinements; vague d-steps admit a menu of
  plausible refinements, and jurisdictions choose differently. A p-step may
  serve more than one d-step (e.g. one site inspection that both assesses
  nuisance and issues the permit). D-step boundaries are defined by p-step
  events, not imposed.
- **Observations** are generated by cases moving through a jurisdiction's
  process model. We never see the process model directly.

This improves on "law-as-code": law-as-code has no room for ambiguity and must
thin the statute to what is executable. Here, ambiguity is explicit, is where
the unknowns live, and is itself something we measure.

The inference problem is therefore mostly *not* "discover the steps from
complaints". It is: given the decision skeleton read from the statute, infer
how each vague step is solidified in each jurisdiction, and what each step
costs the people going through it.

## What we measure

Both the Bayesian model and any LLM baseline must answer the same estimands,
so they can be compared on the same footing.

### 1. Burden, per step, propagated upward

- Measured per **p-step**, then rolled up to its **d-step**, then to the whole
  statute.
- Burden has distinct components that must not be merged: elapsed waiting
  time, applicant hours (effort), and money (fees and required purchases).
  Agency hours are a separate, lower-priority quantity. Steps and money come
  first (see `memory: burden-measurement-priorities`).
- Every estimate carries uncertainty. A p-step whose burden is unknown stays
  unknown; it is never silently zero.
- Shared p-steps are counted once in totals and attributed explicitly to every
  d-step they serve.

### 2. Complexity, of the decision model and of the process models

- **Decision model complexity** (from the text): number of d-steps, number of
  vague d-steps, conditions and branches, deadlines.
- **Process model complexity** (per jurisdiction, inferred): expected p-steps
  executed per case, applicant-facing interactions, distinct actors, branching
  (path entropy, loops such as incomplete-application resubmission), and
  unpredictability (spread of total time).
- **Implementation variety**: how differently jurisdictions solidify each vague
  d-step (entropy across jurisdictions), and the spread in burden that results.

### 3. The link back to drafting: counterfactual statute edits

The estimands above exist so we can predict the effect of drafting choices,
for example:

- make a vague step concrete (specify the procedure);
- add, tighten or loosen a deadline;
- remove or merge a step;
- move a requirement from statute to rule, or vice versa.

For each edit: the predicted change in each burden component, per jurisdiction
and overall, and the change in cross-jurisdiction variation. The "cost of
vagueness" of a clause is the burden and variance attributable to leaving it
vague, compared with the plausible concrete alternatives.

## Method stance

- Read the decision model from text first; it anchors everything else.
- Treat process refinements of vague steps as a bounded hypothesis space
  (a menu), not open-ended discovery. Prefer exact enumeration over the menu
  to sampling over discrete structure.
- LLMs have three principled roles: **prior** (propose the refinement menu and
  weights from statute and regulation text), **measurement** (turn text into
  structured observations, with measured error), and **competitor** (the
  baseline the Bayesian model must beat or usefully complement).
- So far, on real data, the Bayesian model has not beaten the LLM baseline.
  Synthetic worlds with known truth, starting with a very small toy statute,
  are how we find out whether the gap comes from the model, the data or the
  machinery.

## Guiding questions (Alex, 2026-09-28)

1. **The optimal process model under a decision model.** Optimize over *valid*
   processes (those that satisfy the decision model's coverage, ordering,
   deadline and no-extra-requirement constraints), measured by:
   - applicant burden (time, hours, money);
   - agency cost;
   - externalities: missed harms, wrongful denials, and **deterrence**
     (people who never apply, or who proceed without a permit).

   Real-world data can point to a process that no jurisdiction currently
   uses, if its components are observed somewhere (compositional
   extrapolation under the invariance and transport assumptions in
   `docs/process-model-causal-frame.md`). It cannot price genuinely new steps.
2. **Statute perturbations that make a better process achievable for the same
   ends.** A two-level problem:
   - the drafter chooses the text;
   - the text defines the valid process set, and jurisdictions choose within
     it according to a learned behavioural and compliance model;
   - outcomes follow.

   Candidate edits:
   - making a vague procedure concrete;
   - a safe harbour, which makes a vague *standard* concrete;
   - a concurrency clause;
   - deadline redesign (clock start, deemed approval);
   - a consolidated deficiency notice;
   - a publication mandate;
   - fee caps.

   "Same ends" means the protected interest is held fixed, so edits must be
   scored on externalities as well as burden.

**Sub-questions:**
- **One statute can admit several decision models,** through interpretation
  and interaction with other laws. Carry a distribution over readings above
  the process menu.
- **Complexity is not just a corollary of burden.** Keep them as separate
  estimands and study the link:
  - burden is what the process costs people;
  - complexity is structural: openness, implementation variety, path
    entropy, self-assessability, guidance deficit.

  Complexity partly causes burden (errors, learning, variability), and is
  partly a cost in itself (unpredictability, cross-jurisdiction variation).

**Validity vs compliance.** A process can be valid on paper (it satisfies the
text) and still fail in practice: missed deadlines, or "phantom
requirements" not grounded in law. Observed processes are presumptively
valid, not guaranteed valid. Measure compliance separately.

**Needed data or simulation.** Follow-up outcomes measure what each procedure
produces: complaints, enforcement, revocations, appeals, deterrence
(permitted vs actual installations). Untried statutes need simulation built
on estimated components, with uncertainty propagated.

Validity, compliance and the optimal valid design are developed in
`docs/process-validity.md`, with a toy implementation. A first finding is
*defensive proceduralism*: under the vague text, only the heaviest design is
valid under every reading, and a safe harbour makes the cheapest design safe.
For simulating externalities, see the G-Sim note in the same doc.
