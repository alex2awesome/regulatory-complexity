2 · From decision steps to process models

Process models: one per town

Every town writes its own procedure, so each town has its own process model. Towns may also read the vague clauses differently, so each town’s process model implements its own reading of the statute (section 1 shows how the decision tree changes with the reading). Towns can share a design (two towns may both run S3), but the process model belongs to the town. Each is a plain flowchart: the same start (prepare → completeness check, looping through correction if incomplete), the town’s own middle, and the decision. Boxes are coloured by the decision step they carry out, as in the decision tree above. Use ◀ ▶ to step from town to town; each town scales the agency boxes by its own speed, but the box-duration laws are shared.

In S3 and S6 one step — the site inspection — serves two decision steps: it completes the nuisance determination (D2) and issues the permit (D3). Under “notify neighbours”, a hearing happens only if someone objects.

3 · What a case goes through

Average case journey in each town

Each bar is one average case, left to right on a common day scale. Every segment is one process step with its average length; the bands above group steps into the decision step they serve. Loops (corrections) and hearings are averaged in: a step that happens in only some cases shows its average contribution.

W0 has common-sense timings. W1 is built to defy them — backlogged desk reviews, certified-mail notices, a 30-day comment period — to test methods that lean on typical practice.

4 · Is there a best process?

Burden of every process, for every town — including ones no town uses

For each town, the Bayesian model predicts the average days from submission to decision under each candidate process, using that town’s own speed and incompleteness rate. “Concurrent” variants run the D2 steps side by side instead of one after another — a process none of the towns uses. Bars are 90% intervals; dots are the true values.

Bayesian 90% interval (median tick) true value ■ the process this town actually uses

What we can say. On time alone, desk review only (S1) is fastest everywhere, and concurrency recovers most of the time cost of extra safeguards. But “best” is not a time question alone: S1 gathers the least evidence about nuisance (no site visit, no neighbour input), and our data contain nothing about how accurately each procedure detects real nuisances. The model can price the burden of each safeguard; it cannot say whether the safeguard is worth it.
Extrapolation. A town’s own process is estimated tightly; processes it doesn’t use get wide intervals because they borrow step laws from other towns (Alder has never run an inspection, so S2 in Alder borrows Birch’s). This works for recombinations of steps we have observed somewhere — including never-tried arrangements like concurrency — and the intervals still covered the truth. It does not work for genuinely new steps (e.g. online self-certification): nothing in the data says how long those take.

5 · Linking steps to evidence

A step’s burden is measurable only if its boundaries are observed

Observations are intervals between events. An interval measures the sum of the steps between its two events, so whole-process records (submitted → decided) pin down the total and nothing about how it splits. Adding status-change dates splits the chain. Estimands identified from the data in W0:

evidence availableidentified estimands
admin totals (submitted → decided) + applicant reports5
+ status-change dates: deemed complete, inspection, hearing10
+ “review complete” date (the boundary of the vague step)28
+ “notice mailed” date30

When no boundary record exists for steps that always happen together, report their joint burden and treat any split as coming from the prior, not the data.

7 · The two methods

How each method answers

Bayesian process model

A written-down account of how the evidence arises, fitted exactly.

  1. Hypotheses: each town uses one of S1–S6; each case may loop through corrections (0–4 times) and, where possible, a hearing. Every combination is enumerated — no guessing among them.
  2. Unknowns: 36 numbers — each step’s typical length and spread, each town’s speed, incompleteness and objection rates, applicant hours, how noisily people report.
  3. Scoring a guess: for any setting of the unknowns, compute exactly how likely every record is — e.g. “3 weeks from applying to inspection” is scored by adding up the steps between those two events, allowing for rounding and reporting error.
  4. Fitting: a sampler (tempered sequential Monte Carlo) finds all settings consistent with the evidence; every answer is computed for each, and their spread is the uncertainty.

Strong at: identifying each town’s process, using lots of data, honest intervals, consistent numbers (steps add up to totals). Weak at: anything the data can’t pin down, which it fills from a generic prior.

LLM baseline

One call to Claude Opus with no tools, repeated three times and averaged.

  1. Given: the statute, the decision model, the step catalog, the six candidate processes, the same modelling assumptions, and all the evidence as text.
  2. Asked: the same questions, each as a 5th / 50th / 95th percentile, filled into a fixed template.
  3. What it does (inferred from answers): tallies records, reads which steps are mentioned to judge each town’s process, splits totals into steps using general knowledge, and reasons its way to the derived quantities.

Strong at: common-sense scales when data are thin, fast and cheap (~$0.35–$4 per run). Weak at: worlds that break the usual pattern, internal consistency, and calibrated uncertainty.

A hybrid gives the Bayesian model priors elicited from the LLM without showing it the evidence (widened and mixed with the generic prior). It helped at every test so far, but the test of priors that are genuinely wrong for a place — the cultural-transfer concern — is still to come.

8 · The Bayesian model, drawn (current version)

Plate diagram: what depends on what

Arrows run from causes to what they generate. Boxes (“plates”) are repeated: once per agency, per statute, per case, per safeguard. Fitting runs the arrows backwards, from the shaded observations to everything unshaded. This is the model as of the corrected rerun: the text enters through readings, agencies are shared across two statutes, and the observation process includes when a report was posted.

unknown, learned observed unknown, summed out exactly (never sampled) computed from its parents box = repeated for every t, k, i or g

Left: text → reading → design → process → observations

  • Text to readings. The statute text is fixed. Its interpretive questions come from a taxonomy and a blind reader; the prior over readings pools LLM parses and, for threshold words, the RSA model. Each agency’s reading of each statute is a latent, enumerated exactly, shifted by that agency’s strictness and its density.
  • Readings to designs. A reading fixes which designs are legal (validity rules); the agency picks among them by burden and its cost sensitivity τ, with slack ε for practice no reading explains. The design is enumerated, never sampled.
  • Agencies shared across statutes. Pace, error rate, strictness and τ belong to the agency, not the statute: the same 14 agencies implement both laws.
  • Observations. Loops and durations are summed out exactly; a report’s posting day decides whether it appears completed or pending, and that selection sits inside the sum over latent durations (the audit’s F2 fix).
  • Size. 63–70 sampled numbers per statute fit; designs, readings, paths and durations are all integrated exactly.

Right: outcomes

  • Burden sets how many would-be keepers apply; the design’s safeguards set how often a nuisance is caught or a harmless coop flagged; nuisance share and arrivals are per agency.
  • Expected counts are computed for disjoint person-level cells. Denials and unpermitted complaints overlap (a denied applicant may keep hens and be complained about), so their joint likelihood is computed exactly rather than as independent Poissons (the audit’s F1 fix).
  • Outcomes are still fitted conditional on saved process draws (a cut), so a mistake in this layer cannot contaminate the burden results; joint fitting is a later step.

This diagram is the whole model: nothing enters the fit that is not drawn here. The LLM baseline has no such diagram. It reads the same evidence and answers the same questions, and its internal reasoning is not constrained to be consistent with any single generative story. 9 · Is a process legal?

Validity under a reading of the statute

A process is valid under a reading if it covers every required determination, gathers evidence the reading accepts, makes the determination before the permit issues, can fit its own mandated waits inside the clock, imposes only burdens a clause authorizes, and denies only on authorized grounds. Vague clauses raise yes/no questions (does a desk review suffice? must neighbours be heard? may the town require inspection access? does the clock run through the determination? are separate fees allowed?); a reading is one answer to each. Validity is not compliance: in W0 the towns run plausibly valid designs and still miss the 30-day clock 3%, 48% and 55% of the time.

Defensive proceduralism. Under the vague text only the notice-and-hearing design is safe under every reading, so a town that wants legal safety is pushed to the heaviest procedure. A one-line safe harbour (“a coop ≥ 10 ft from every property line is deemed not a nuisance”) makes the fastest design safe: about 5–7 estimated days per application saved (10–11 in truth), plus the hearing hours. Percentages use an illustrative prior over readings; the “every reading” results use none.

10 · What each process produces

Outcomes: nuisances caught, applicants deterred

An outcome layer adds what the burden numbers leave out: each coop is a nuisance or not; each safeguard (desk review, inspection, neighbour comment) catches a real nuisance with some rate and flags a harmless coop with another; burden deters would-be applicants, some of whom keep hens anyway; complaints and a household survey are observed. The Bayesian model fits this with an exact likelihood for the count cells, taking burden estimates from the process fit.

Reading it. Differences between designs are recovered well even where absolute levels are not; a heavier process buys real safety in one world and almost none in the other. Per step, the ledger says what each step costs and catches: in Birch an inspection added to desk review costs +37 day-equivalents and prevents 3.4 nuisances per 100, but only 1.7 when notice is already in place, because safeguards overlap. Caveat: these fits used an outcome likelihood the audit (section 14) found to mis-handle overlapping counts; they are being refit and the deterrence-bias finding is suspended until then.

11 · Reading the law is part of the model

From text to decision model, with uncertainty

Earlier rounds hand-coded the decision model from the statute. Round 4 made the reading a latent variable. Three nested trees: an interpretive tree (branch on each yes/no question; each leaf is one reading, i.e. one decision model), a decision tree per reading (the required determinations in order), and the process tree of section 2 per decision model. Burden and outcomes attach to the leaves and roll up; the difference between the sub-trees under a question’s two answers is the cost of leaving that clause vague.

The inference runs backwards from practice: a town’s process is evidence about which reading its agency acts on, given a model of how the agency chooses among legal designs (any legal design; the cheapest; the legally safest). Readings are identified only relative to that choice model, and only where practice can differ: in the 12-town world, two of five questions leave no trace in any design.

12 · The two models behind the reading layer

Noah Goodman’s model of vagueness, and inverse planning

Vagueness as an uncertain threshold (Lassiter & Goodman 2017)

A vague word like “nuisance” is true of a coop when its nuisance degree x exceeds a threshold θ that nobody states. The model treats θ as an ordinary uncertain variable.

  • Literal listener L0(x | u, θ) ∝ P(x)·[[u]](x; θ): takes the word at face value for a given threshold.
  • Speaker S1(u | x, θ) ∝ exp(α·(log L0 − cost)): chooses words that inform a literal listener, at a cost.
  • Pragmatic listener L1(x, θ | u) ∝ S1·P(x)·P(θ): hears “nuisance” and infers the degree and the threshold jointly.

The threshold posterior concentrates where the word would have been worth saying, in the upper part of P(x); move P(x) up (a denser town) and θ moves up. So a reading of §3 is a threshold setting, readings are a distribution, and the model predicts systematic variation with local conditions. Status: implemented faithfully on a nuisance-degree grid; used only as one prior source for one question; contributed one number in round 4 and did not help there. Its use for a legal rule rather than a single object is our reinterpretation, still untested.

Inverse planning (Baker, Saxe & Tenenbaum 2009)

Forward planning predicts actions from goals and beliefs; inverse planning observes the action and infers goals and beliefs: P(goal, belief | action) ∝ P(action | goal, belief)·P(goal, belief), with a rational planner as the likelihood.

  • Belief = the agency’s reading of the statute (with the vagueness model shaping its prior).
  • Goal = how it chooses among legal designs: cheapest, safest, any.
  • Action = the process design, which we never see directly.
  • Observations = records, reports and web pages that each span some of the design’s steps; the process model (step laws, town speed) is the “world dynamics” that turns actions into observations.

The whole chain composes the two inversions: the legislature is the speaker, the agency is the listener and the planner, and we invert the agency from partial observations of what it built. The catch, made explicit by the framework: goals and readings are confounded from one snapshot; separating them needs the same agency under changed constraints, which is why the linked two-statute worlds (section 15) exist.

13 · A second statute, read blind

Can the questions be discovered, not hand-written?

An Opus “author” agent wrote a second statute (a home-kitchen food-business registration act) with seven hidden interpretive questions, a 12-design space, data-form validity rules and a truth block. A separate Opus “reader” saw only the public text and catalog, and proposed its own questions and per-design validity judgments.

  • The reader recovered 6 of the 7 hidden questions as major, demoted one (fee scope) to minor, and added one the author had not posed (must the health authority itself check the referenced act?).
  • Its validity judgments agree with the author’s on 62.5% of reading × design cells. The main divergence is a real question about agency discretion: the reader treats unrequired procedure as unauthorized, the author treats it as permitted.
  • The LLM baseline on the resulting worlds identified 13–14 of 14 designs and beat the reader’s prior on readings; where fees are generated conditional on the fee reading it learns that question from fee schedules (Brier 0.25 → 0.08), an information path the Bayesian model does not yet have.

The code is now statute-agnostic: both statutes load from a package (text, catalog, design space, rules), and the hen statute reproduces exactly through the loader. The Bayesian fits on the second statute are part of the corrected rerun.

14 · Independent audit

What was wrong, and what changed

An independent agent audited the whole implementation. Its main findings were correct, and all are fixed with counterexample tests:

  • Outcome likelihood multiplied Poisson terms for overlapping counts (a denied applicant can reappear in the unpermitted-complaint counts). Replaced by the exact joint.
  • Applicant reports omitted the fact that only cases finished by the posting day appear as completed; long cases are more likely pending. The selection factor now sits inside the latent-duration sum (verified against brute force).
  • FFT sums could wrap long durations (three 400-day steps landed at day 176). Grid padded; tails clipped, not renormalized.
  • Scoring counted unknown-reading mass as “no”, and included an unlearnable question in the headline. Both corrected; section 11 shows the corrected numbers.
  • Identification: residual-reading mass and compliance slack are exactly confounded; only their combination is learnable. The risk-preference model was absurdly scaled. Claims re-labelled or withdrawn.

Consequence: every fit on this page before section 15 used the earlier likelihood. Design identification and the sampler-precision conclusions stand; per-step numbers and the outcome and reading results are superseded by the rerun once it lands.

15 · Where things are

Shared agencies, and the corrected rerun

To separate what an agency believes from what it wants, the same 14 agencies now implement both statutes. Each agency has fixed traits (pace, applicant-error rate, cost sensitivity, and an interpretive strictness that shifts every strict-vs-permissive question the same way) that generate a hen world and a home-kitchen world. The first test is whether an agency’s pace posterior agrees across statutes; pooled learning of the traits is the next round.

Running: six fits on the corrected likelihood at 10,000 particles (the 3-town baselines, the 12-town hen world, the second statute, and the two linked worlds). Results will replace the caveated numbers above.

16 · The model in equations

RSA, inverse planning, and our full model

The inference, in five steps

∏towns t p(decision modelt | text) · p(process modelt | decision modelt, agency goals) · p(observationst | process modelt, step durations)
One process model per town (towns may also read the statute differently). In the notation below: ∏t P(rt | text) · P(St | rt, ψ) · P(yt | St, θ).
  1. Text → candidate decision models. An LLM (or a person) reads the statute and writes the open interpretive questions (“must neighbours be heard?”). Every combination of answers is one candidate decision model, plus a residual “none of these”. Prior probabilities come from LLM elicitation, pooled parses, or the RSA threshold model.
  2. Decision model → lawful process models. For each candidate decision model, validity rules mark which process designs are lawful under it; each town then picks one of them as its own process model. A choice model (any lawful design / cheapest / legally safest, with a little slack) turns that into p(process model | decision model).
  3. Observations → each town’s process model (Stage B). Sampling fits the step-duration laws and town traits to the records, reports and web pages, and gives each town a likelihood for every candidate process design. This step does not use the text at all.
  4. Combine (Stage C). Exact reweighting multiplies the three pieces: each town’s posterior over decision models and process designs, a shared population share of readings across towns, and a comparison of agency-goal settings by marginal likelihood.
  5. Answer. Burden per step rolled up to decision steps and the statute, complexity, and counterfactual edits, all averaged over what is still uncertain.

Step 4 does not pick one winning decision model. It gives a distribution per town, because towns may read the statute differently, and a question whose answer leaves no trace in the data stays at its prior.

RSA: vagueness as an uncertain threshold (Lassiter & Goodman)

PL0(state | word, threshold) ∝ P(state) · 1[state > threshold]
PS1(word | state, threshold) ∝ exp( α · [ log PL0(state | word, threshold) − cost(word) ] )
PL1(state, threshold | word) ∝ PS1(word | state, threshold) · P(state) · P(threshold)
The literal listener takes the word at face value for a given threshold; the speaker picks words that lead a literal listener to the true state; the pragmatic listener hears the word and infers both the state and the unspoken threshold. α = speaker rationality.

Inverse planning (Baker, Saxe & Tenenbaum)

P(goals, beliefs | actions) ∝ P(goals, beliefs) · P(actions | goals, beliefs)
P(actions | goals, beliefs) ∝ exp( rationality · value of the actions for those goals, given those beliefs )
The agent is assumed to choose roughly the best actions for what it wants, given what it believes; the observer inverts this to recover goals and beliefs from actions.

Towns can read the statute differently. Each town t has its own reading rt. Readings are drawn from a shared population share w, and κ sets how alike towns are: large κ means towns mostly follow the population; small κ lets each town go its own way. What is shared across towns is the space of questions the text raises, not the answers.

How it is computed. θ is sampled first (Stage B: tempered SMC with a uniform prior over designs). r, S and w are then integrated exactly by reweighting those θ particles (Stage C). ψ is not sampled: it sits on a fixed grid (uniform; cost τ = 15, 40; risk β = 2, 5; ε = 0.05, 0.15, 0.30), and settings are compared by log marginal likelihood. So “learning the agency’s goals” currently means choosing among a few candidate ψ shared by all towns; a per-agency ψt is what the linked worlds HEN2/HK2 are for (round 7). ρ and ε are exactly confounded when towns are independent, so ρ is held at 0.05.

Two planners, one observer

There are two planners in the chain. The legislature plans the text: in RSA terms it is the speaker, choosing words by simulating how a reader will act on them. Each town’s agency plans its process: it is the listener that forms a reading, then an inverse-planning agent that picks a lawful design. We are the observer, inverting the agencies from partial data. The legislature’s planning problem is the drafting question itself: it is what the counterfactual edits evaluate, forward, using the fitted agency model.

Two departures: we do not use the pragmatic listener (the agency is not modelled as reasoning about why the legislature chose its words), and actions are latent and one-shot rather than observed sequences.

The questions, and who answers them

The questions a reader asks of this statute

A reader interprets the law by listing what the text leaves open, as yes/no questions. Each town’s answers are its reading, and they decide which process designs are lawful. These are the author’s six questions for the hen statute (v2), with the author’s prior probability of “yes”. Six yes/no questions give 26 = 64 readings, hence 64 possible decision models. Only Q1–Q3 change which designs are lawful, so only they can be learned from what towns do; Q4 changes timing, Q5 only fee schedules, and Q6 leaves no trace. S4 and S9 are lawful under every reading, which is why a cautious town picks them.

Who reads the statute, and what they choose

A reading is one set of answers to the questions the text leaves open; with the text fixed, each reading gives exactly one decision model. Two different parties “read”. Our readers (the author, LLMs, blind readers) only write down the space: which questions are open, what each answer makes lawful, and a prior. Each town’s agency is the real reader: it makes two choices, and we see neither directly.
